

SLIDE 2 — Why sampling-based motion planning?  (~45 sec)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Generally, sampling based planners arise in problems where we are limited by the computational complexity and optimization landscape presented by some robotic navigation task.
Sampling-based planners aim to sidestep both of these problems. The particular method I will be discussing today and my own variant of is the Rapidly-exploring Random
Tree — RRT, which grows a tree from the start by repeatedly sampling a random
configuration, finding its nearest neighbor in the tree,
and steering toward. The result is a space-filling tree
that's fast to build and probabilistically complete.

The catch is that vanilla RRT samples uniformly from free space and operates with no model of a navigation goal. 


================================================================================

SLIDE 3 — Prior work biases toward a known goal  (~40 sec)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A natural idea to incorporate knowledge of the goal is to direct a bias towards the goal in the our sampls. Goal-biased RRT does this literally — with some probability p,
you sampling the goal location directly instead of a
random point. RRT-Connect goes further and grows two trees simultaneously, one
from the start and one from the goal, connecting them in the middle. Informed
RRT-star is more principled: once you have any path, you restrict future samples
to the ellipsoidal region that could possibly improve it.

All three of these are better than uniform RRT but they all
assume that the goal is a known, fixed point in free space. In practice,
that assumption breaks down in several safety critical scenarios such as search and rescue or autonomous exploration


================================================================================

SLIDE 4 — Environments: Procedurally Generated Mazes  (~35 sec)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

For evaluation we needed environments with controllable difficulty and varied
topology, so we built a procedural maze generator. The algorithm runs a random
walk over a discrete grid — ten-by-ten cells for easy, twenty-by-twenty for
medium, thirty-by-thirty for hard.

The key implementation point is that those grid cells never appear in the actual
planner. The maze walls get converted to rectangular polygons in a
continuous hundred-by-hundred unit coordinate space, and the RRT samples from
that continuous space using exact geometric collision checks.



================================================================================

SLIDE 5 — The system, end-to-end  (~40 sec)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

So now that we know what environment we're operating in, let's look at how the
full system fits together before we go into any individual component.

The planner runs as a continuous loop across five stages. The robot takes a
noisy distance observation to the goal during the sense step. That observation
feeds a Kalman filter update, which refines the Gaussian belief over goal
location. The updated belief then feeds the sampler, which is where the core
contribution lives. The sampler then produces candidate
configurations, the RRT extends the tree toward them, and the robot executes a
partial path before the loop repeats.

A few implementation details worth flagging: The MCMC chain runs
Metropolis-Hastings targeting a Boltzmann distribution at temperature ten 
that distribution is defined by the cost function we'll see later.

Additionally, loop doesn't replan on every step. It replans only when the entropy of the
belief distribution drops past a threshold, which is the trigger condition shown at the
bottom.


================================================================================

SLIDE 6 — The sampler: lambda biasing  (~45 sec)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

At each iteration we need a candidate configuration to grow the RRT toward. With probability one-minus-lambda we draw
from an MCMC chain, and with probability lambda we draw uniformly at random from
free space. In our experiments lambda is fixed at 0.30.

The reason you need the mix comes down to probabilistic completeness. Pure MCMC
concentrates its mass around the current belief mean. If that estimate is wrong early in the mission, the chain gets stuck, and the planner can fail even when a valid path exists.
The thirty percent uniform component prevents this and guarantees the
tree will eventually reach any reachable configuration regardless of where the
belief is pointing.

On the MCMC side: the chain runs Metropolis-Hastings with a Gaussian proposal, and the target distribution is Boltzmann — pi of q. The chain
state is preserved between planning rounds so it doesn't restart cold.


================================================================================

SLIDE 7 — Information-aware cost function  (~45 sec)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

That Boltzmann distribution the MCMC chain targets is defined by this cost
function that we are about to unpack.

J of q has three terms. The first is just Euclidean distance to the belief mean
mu. This is convex with a global minimum at mu, which produces the well-behavied gradient landscape necessary for MCMC methods.

The second term is a clearance penalty, which creates a
repulsive potential that grows as the robot approaches walls. Anything in
collision or out of bounds gets assigned infinite cost.

The third term is where the active perception comes in. For any candidate
configuration q, we simulate what would happen if the robot took a sensor reading
from there. We then run a hypothetical Kalman update and compute how much the total
variance of the belief would shrink. That expected reduction in variance is the
information gain. We subtract it from the cost, so high-information positions are
cheaper to visit. 



================================================================================

SLIDE 8 — Goal belief: Kalman-style updates  (~35 sec)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The cost function we just saw depends on the current belief over goal location, which we are now going to address the mechanics of.

We represent it as a Gaussian: mean mu is our best estimate of where the goal
is, covariance Sigma captures our uncertainty. At each step the robot takes a
range observation  and runs a standard Kalman
update.

The noise model is distance-dependent: variance scales linearly with distance, so
observations taken close to the goal are more informative than ones taken far
away. 

Two properties worth noting: covariance reduction is monotonic, so the belief
can only sharpen over time, never widen. Second, the module is fully decoupled from
the RRT — it just takes observations in and emits an updated Gaussian. The
convergence plot on the right shows this playing out across all ninety MCMC-agent
trials.


================================================================================

SLIDE 9 — Receding horizon execution  (~35 sec)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

With the belief module in place, the last architectural piece is deciding when
to replan.

The naive approach is to replan on every step — every time you take an
observation, throw away the current plan and compute a new one. That's
expensive: each planning episode runs up to fifteen thousand RRT iterations with
Shapely collision checks at a third of a millisecond each. 

Instead we use an entropy trigger. After each observation we compute the Shannon
entropy of the belief. If it dropped by more than a threshold we replan. If the belief
didn't change much, we keep executing the current plan.

The bar chart on the right shows the practical consequence: easy mazes average
about five and a half replans per trial, medium around eight, and hard around
thirteen and a half. 


================================================================================

SLIDE 10 — Empirical evaluation: 180 trials  (~40 sec)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

 We ran 180 trials total — thirty per
agent per difficulty level — tracking success rate, computation time, path
length, and replan count.

MCMC gets sixty-three percent on hard mazes, baseline RRT gets ninety-three.
Across all difficulty levels, MCMC is eight to twenty-two times slower.
On easy and medium mazes the success rates are comparable, and on those difficulties the MCMC agent
does find shorter paths — statistically significant via Mann-Whitney. 



================================================================================

SLIDE 11 — What we learned  (~40 sec)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------


On the left you can see what worked. The belief integration is solid — covariance
converges in every single trial, no exceptions. Lambda biasing provably helps:
we ran ablations with pure MCMC and the local-minimum failure rate went up
significantly. The information gain term does guide the tree toward sensing
useful positions. And on the mazes where the system succeeds, and the paths are
genuinely shorter.

The failures are on the right, and they're mostly about hyperparameters that
weren't tuned per difficulty. The entropy threshold that triggers replanning is
the biggest one: on hard mazes, thirteen-plus replans at fifteen thousand
iterations each is simply too much. A coarser trigger, or an adaptive one that
loosens as the maze gets harder, would help substantially. Similarly, lambda
of zero point three and temperature ten were fixed across all experiments and
hard mazes likely want a colder chain and more uniform exploration early on.

The path length figure on the right gives the clearest view of where the signal
is. On easy and medium, the MCMC bars are shorter. On hard, they disappear
because the success rate drops.


================================================================================

SLIDE 12 — What's reusable  (~35 sec)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The aggregate result is mixed, but I want to close by making clear parts of this project 
that interested individuals could use for their own work, again found on my Github.

The MCMC sampler is a self-contained module — Metropolis-Hastings with a lambda
mix and a swappable cost function. Drop it into any RRT-style planner and it
will bias sampling toward wherever your cost function says is interesting.

The goal-belief module is also fully decoupled. It's a Kalman filter with
distance-dependent noise, and it outputs a Gaussian. Any planner that needs to
localize a target while executing can plug it in directly.

The evaluation harness ran all 180 trials with statistical tests baked in including —
Mann-Whitney, chi-square, Cohen's d — and logged full telemetry per trial. That
infrastructure is reusable for any future comparison on this benchmark.
