Slides 5–7 Script

SLIDE 5 — The system, end-to-end  (~40 sec)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

So now that we know what environment we're operating in, let's look at how the
full system fits together before we go into any individual component.

The planner runs as a continuous loop across five stages. The robot takes a
noisy distance observation to the goal — that's the sense step. That observation
feeds a Kalman filter update, which refines the Gaussian belief over goal
location. The updated belief then feeds the sampler, which is where the core
contribution lives — we'll get to that next. The sampler produces candidate
configurations, the RRT extends the tree toward them, and the robot executes a
partial path before the loop repeats.

A few implementation details worth flagging: collision checking is done via
Shapely polygon intersection at about 0.33 milliseconds per query,
nearest-neighbor search uses a scipy KD-tree at O(log n), and each planning
episode runs up to fifteen thousand RRT iterations. The MCMC chain runs
Metropolis-Hastings targeting a Boltzmann distribution at temperature ten —
that distribution is defined by the cost function we'll see on slide seven.

The loop doesn't replan on every step. It replans only when the entropy of the
belief distribution drops past a threshold — meaning the robot learned something
meaningful about where the goal is. That's the trigger condition shown at the
bottom.


SLIDE 6 — The sampler: lambda biasing  (~45 sec)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The sampler is the central engineering contribution, so let's be precise about
what it does.

At each iteration we need a candidate configuration to grow the RRT toward. The
formula at the top is how we get it: with probability one-minus-lambda we draw
from an MCMC chain, and with probability lambda we draw uniformly at random from
free space. In our experiments lambda is fixed at 0.30.

The reason you need the mix comes down to probabilistic completeness. Pure MCMC
concentrates its mass around the current belief mean — wherever we think the
goal is. If that estimate is wrong early in the mission, the chain gets stuck,
the tree stops exploring, and the planner can fail even when a valid path exists.
The thirty percent uniform component is what prevents that. It guarantees the
tree will eventually reach any reachable configuration regardless of where the
belief is pointing.

On the MCMC side: the chain runs Metropolis-Hastings with a Gaussian proposal at
standard deviation five, and the target distribution is Boltzmann — pi of q
proportional to exp of negative J of q over T, with temperature ten. The chain
state is preserved between planning rounds so it doesn't restart cold. The
practical payoff is that once the belief converges and the chain is mixing well
near the true goal region, you can tune lambda down and get more focused sampling
— it's a knob that tracks uncertainty.


SLIDE 7 — Information-aware cost function  (~45 sec)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

That Boltzmann distribution the MCMC chain targets is defined by this cost
function — so let's unpack it.

J of q has three terms. The first is just Euclidean distance to the belief mean
mu. This is convex with a global minimum at mu, which is exactly what you want
for MCMC — no gradient reversals, no saddle points, the chain mixes cleanly.
Weight lambda-g is one.

The second term is a clearance penalty: lambda-c over d-obs of q plus epsilon,
where d-obs is the minimum distance to any obstacle boundary. This creates a
repulsive potential that grows as the robot approaches walls. Anything in
collision or out of bounds gets assigned infinite cost — a hard constraint.
Weight lambda-c is zero point five.

The third term is where the active perception idea lives. For any candidate
configuration q, we simulate what would happen if the robot took a sensor reading
from there — we run a hypothetical Kalman update and compute how much the total
variance of the belief would shrink. That expected reduction in det-Sigma is the
information gain. We subtract it from the cost, so high-information positions are
cheaper to visit. Weight lambda-i is two.

The net effect is that the sampler naturally trades off three things at once: get
closer to the likely goal, stay away from walls, and prefer positions where
looking would actually tell you something. As the belief converges and Sigma
shrinks, the third term vanishes on its own — no manual schedule needed.
