SLIDE 2 — Why sampling-based motion planning?  (~45 sec)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The setting is standard robot motion planning: given a continuous configuration
space — in our case the 2D plane — find a collision-free path from a start to a
goal. Grid-based methods are the obvious first attempt, but they scale
exponentially with dimension and break down when obstacle geometry is irregular.
Trajectory optimization is faster but gets trapped in local minima whenever the
obstacle set is non-convex, which is essentially always in real environments.

Sampling-based planners sidestep both problems. The Rapidly-exploring Random
Tree — RRT — grows a tree from the start by repeatedly sampling a random
configuration, finding its nearest neighbor in the tree with a KD-tree lookup,
and steering toward it by a fixed step size. The result is a space-filling tree
that's fast to build and, in the limit, probabilistically complete.

The catch — and this is the starting point for everything that follows — is that
vanilla RRT samples uniformly from free space. It has no model of where the goal
is, and no way to exploit prior information. That's fine if the goal is a known
point. It becomes a problem when the goal is uncertain.


SLIDE 3 — Prior work biases toward a known goal  (~40 sec)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Once you accept that uniform sampling is inefficient, the natural fix is to bias
toward the goal. Goal-biased RRT does this literally — with some probability p,
usually five to ten percent, you sample the goal location directly instead of a
random point. RRT-Connect goes further and grows two trees simultaneously, one
from the start and one from the goal, connecting them in the middle. Informed
RRT-star is more principled: once you have any path, you restrict future samples
to the ellipsoidal region that could possibly improve it.

All three of these are better than uniform RRT — but they all share a hidden
assumption: that the goal is a known, fixed point in free space. In practice,
that assumption breaks in exactly the situations where you most want a planner.
Search and rescue: you know roughly where a person is from a radio signal, but
not exactly. Autonomous exploration: your sensor flagged a region of interest,
but the estimate has noise. In every case, what you actually have is a
distribution over the goal location — and none of the standard methods know what
to do with that.

Our angle: represent the goal as a Gaussian belief, update it from noisy range
observations as the robot moves, and build a sampler that acts on the belief
rather than a fixed point. The right panel shows the structure — we'll get into
the mechanics next.


SLIDE 4 — Environments: Procedurally Generated Mazes  (~35 sec)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

For evaluation we needed environments with controllable difficulty and varied
topology, so we built a procedural maze generator. The algorithm runs a random
walk over a discrete grid — ten-by-ten cells for easy, twenty-by-twenty for
medium, thirty-by-thirty for hard — carving passages as it goes. That gives you
the connectivity structure.

The key implementation point is that those grid cells never appear in the actual
planner. The maze walls get converted to rectangular Shapely polygons in a
continuous hundred-by-hundred unit coordinate space, and the RRT samples from
that continuous space using exact geometric collision checks — not lookups into
a grid.

You can see the three difficulty levels here: easy has open corridors and few
decision points, medium starts creating bottlenecks, and hard has dense walls
with many narrow passages that are easy to miss. Start is always bottom-left,
goal is always top-right, and crucially — the planner only ever receives a noisy
distance estimate to the goal, not its location.
